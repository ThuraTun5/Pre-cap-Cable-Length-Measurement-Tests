74LVC245 level shifter

After this page you can explain why the encoder can't connect straight to the ESP32, wire the 74LVC245 correctly (including the pins you don't use), check the logic levels with numbers, and read the T03 test output.

I assumed: you know what HIGH/LOW logic levels are, and you did T01 (setup/loop, millis). Goal = understand T03 while you test it.

OverviewMap of this test

The encoder speaks "5 V logic". The ESP32 speaks "3.3 V logic". The 74LVC245 sits between them and translates in one direction: A side (5 V in) → B side (3.3 V out).

Section 1Why the encoder can't connect straight to the ESP32

Why this matters: it is the one mistake in this project that can kill the ESP32 without any smoke or warning.

The AMT102 runs on 5 V, and its HIGH output is at least VDD − 0.8 = 4.2 V, up to 5 V. The ESP32 runs on 3.3 V, and its absolute maximum is 3.6 V on any pin.

Inside each ESP32 pin there are small protection diodes to its 3.3 V supply. If you put 5 V on the pin, current flows through that diode into the 3.3 V supply. Sometimes it works for a while; then the pin or the whole chip fails. Extra

In one sentence: 5 V on a 3.3 V pin is outside the ESP32's ratings, so every encoder signal goes through the level shifter first.

Section 2How the 74LVC245 does it

Why this matters: once you see where the output voltage comes from, you understand why pin 20 must be 3.3 V and never 5 V.

That's why the chip is a level shifter: the input accepts 5 V, but the output takes its HIGH level from the chip's own supply. Power the chip from 3.3 V and every output is at most 3.3 V.

In one sentence: the output HIGH is the chip's VCC, so VCC = 3.3 V gives safe 3.3 V outputs.

Small details "Inputs accept voltages to 5.5 V" (TI). The A pins have no diode to VCC that would leak the 5 V into the 3.3 V supply. That is what makes them "5 V tolerant". The chip also has "Ioff": when it is unpowered, 5 V on its pins does not damage it or back-power it.

In one sentence: VCC 3.3 V, DIR HIGH, OE LOW: the chip copies A → B at 3.3 V levels, all the time.

Section 4Unused inputs and the 100 nF capacitor

Why this matters: these two "extra" connections are the most common thing beginners leave out, and the chip can then misbehave in random ways.

Unused inputs (A4–A8 → GND). A CMOS input connected to nothing "floats": it picks up noise from your hand and from nearby wires, and can sit halfway between LOW and HIGH. At halfway, both transistors inside the input are partly on. The chip then draws extra current, can get warm, and the output can oscillate. TI's rule: "All unused inputs of the device must be held at VCC or GND." We use GND.

The 100 nF capacitor (pins 20 and 19/10). Every time an output switches, the chip needs a short, fast pulse of current. Long breadboard wires can't deliver it fast enough, so the supply voltage at the chip dips for a moment. A small capacitor right at the chip acts as a tiny local battery for these pulses. Ceramic, marked "104", any orientation.

In one sentence: never leave an input floating, and give every logic chip a 100 nF capacitor at its supply pins.

Section 5 · the hard ideaLogic levels and noise margins

Why this matters: "it works" is not enough for a measuring machine. Noise margins tell you how much electrical noise (motors!) the signal can take before a count is lost.

Each input has a LOW zone, a HIGH zone, and an "undefined" zone in between. A signal is safe when the sender's worst output is inside the receiver's zone, with distance to spare. That distance is the noise margin:

\[ NM_H = V_{OH,\min}(\text{sender}) - V_{IH}(\text{receiver}) \qquad NM_L = V_{IL}(\text{receiver}) - V_{OL,\max}(\text{sender}) \]

LinkVOH min → VIHNMHVOL max → VILNML
AMT102 (5 V) → 74LVC245 A4.2 V → 2.0 V2.2 V0.4 V → 0.8 V0.4 V
74LVC245 B (3.3 V) → ESP323.1 V → 2.475 V0.625 V0.2 V → 0.825 V0.625 V

ESP32: VIH = 0.75 × 3.3 V, VIL = 0.25 × 3.3 V (datasheet Table 15). 74LVC245 VOH/VOL at a small load (the ESP32 input draws only nanoamps).

Section 6Is the chip fast enough for the encoder?

The encoder has 2048 pulses per turn, and the 20-tooth pulley moves 40 mm per turn. If someone pushes the slider fast, say 0.5 m/s:

\[ f = \frac{v}{40\ \text{mm}} \times 2048 = \frac{500\ \text{mm/s}}{40\ \text{mm}} \times 2048 = 25.6\ \text{kHz} \]

The time between two edges (A or B) is a quarter period, about 10 µs at 0.5 m/s. The chip delays each edge by at most 6.3 ns, which is under 0.1 % of that time. It also delays A and B equally, so the order of the edges (which tells the direction) stays correct.

In one sentence: the 74LVC245 is roughly 1000 times faster than the encoder needs, even for a fast hand.

Small details The encoder itself is the limit: at 2048 PPR the datasheet allows 7500 RPM = 125 turns/s = 5 m/s of slider speed. A hand won't get close. Extra

Section 7The ESP32 side and the T03 code

Why this matters: these are the exact pins and the same reading pattern T4 builds on.

Pins. GPIO34 and GPIO35 are input-only and have no internal pull-up. That's fine, because the 245 actively drives them HIGH or LOW. GPIO32 is a normal pin, and we also set it to plain INPUT.

Why not GPIO39? While researching T3 we found Espressif errata 3.11: GPIO36 and GPIO39 can be pulled LOW for about 80 ns when the ESP32's ADC powers up, and the Wi-Fi driver uses that ADC. For the encoder's index pulse that could fake a "zero" mark. So encoder X moved to GPIO32, which is also next to 34 and 35 on the board.

Debounce. When you move a jumper by hand, the contact bounces and floats for a few milliseconds. The program reads all three pins every 1 ms, but accepts a new level only after it has stayed the same for 20 ms. So each move prints exactly one line.

bool level = digitalRead(ch.pin) == HIGH;
if (level != ch.raw) {            // raw reading changed?
  ch.raw = level;
  ch.rawSinceMs = now;            // start the "how long stable" clock
  ch.rawChanges++;                // count for the stability check
}
if (ch.raw != ch.stable && now - ch.rawSinceMs >= DEBOUNCE_MS) {
  ch.stable = ch.raw;             // stable for 20 ms: accept it
  Serial.printf("%s : %s -> %s\n", ch.name, levelText(!ch.stable), levelText(ch.stable));
  showRgb();                      // red = A1, green = A2, blue = A3
}

Stability warning. Once per second the program checks how often each raw reading changed. More than 20 changes in a second means a loose wire or a floating input. Then it prints WARNING … changes in 1 s. This is a cheap way to find bad breadboard contacts.

Note: this 20 ms debounce is only for hand-moved jumpers. In T4 the encoder signals are counted in hardware (PCNT), which has its own tiny glitch filter in nanoseconds. A 20 ms debounce would throw away real encoder pulses.

In one sentence: the ESP32 reads 35/34/32, accepts a level only when it is stable, and warns you about bad contacts.

PracticeWorked example: check one link by numbers

Problem. A student powers the 74LVC245 from 5 V by mistake (pin 20 on the 5V rail). (a) What voltage does B1 give when A1 is HIGH? (b) Is that safe for GPIO35? (c) With the correct 3.3 V supply, what is the HIGH noise margin from B1 to the ESP32?

a

Why: the output HIGH comes from the chip's own VCC (section 2).

B1 ≈ VCC = 5 V. Also, the chip is only rated for VCC up to 3.6 V, so it is outside its own ratings too.

b

Why: compare with the ESP32 absolute maximum.

5 V > 3.6 V (ESP32 absolute maximum) → not safe. The level shifter would do nothing useful.

c

Why: the noise margin is the worst sender output minus the receiver's threshold.

\( NM_H = V_{OH,\min} - V_{IH} = 3.1\ \text{V} - 0.75 \times 3.3\ \text{V} = 3.1 - 2.475 = 0.625\ \text{V} \)

Sanity check. In Part A you should measure B1 ≈ 3.2–3.3 V, a bit above the 3.1 V minimum, as expected with almost no load. If you ever measure about 5 V on pin 18, pin 20 is on the wrong rail.

Watch outTest traps

  • Pin 20 on 5 V. The outputs become 5 V and the shifting is gone. Check pin 18 with the meter in Part A before connecting any B pin to the ESP32.
  • Chip turned around. If the notch is at the bottom, pin 1 is where you think pin 11 is. The notch points away from you, or up in the diagram.
  • Counting the right side downwards. The right side counts up: pin 20 is top-right, pin 11 bottom-right. B1 is pin 18, not pin 12.
  • DIR left floating or LOW. Then the A pins can become outputs and fight the encoder. DIR goes to 3.3 V.
  • OE forgotten. Floating OE gives outputs that switch on and off at random. OE goes to GND.
  • Unused A4–A8 left open. Strange current draw and warm chip. Tie them to GND.
  • A B pin or ESP32 pin touching the 5V rail. Only A1–A3 may touch the 5V rail.
  • Thinking the input-only pins need a pull-up. They can't have one, and they don't need one: the 245 drives them.
  • Using GPIO36/39 for encoder signals. Errata 3.11. We use 34/35/32.
