Stepper driver basics

After this page you can explain how a stepper motor turns, how STEP pulses become rpm, why the driver sets current (not voltage), and why the three safety rules exist.

Short page for someone new to stepper motors.

OverviewWho does what

The ESP32 never powers the motor. It only tells the driver what to do: settings once over UART, then one STEP pulse for every small step, and DIR for the direction. The driver takes the energy from the 24 V supply.

Section 1How a stepper turns

Inside the motor there are two coils (A and B) and a magnet rotor with many teeth. The driver switches current through the coils in a fixed order, and the rotor jumps to the next position each time: 200 full steps per turn (1.8° each, from the motor's datasheet).

The motor holds its position as long as current flows, even standing still. That's why the shaft is hard to turn after e, and free again after x.

In one sentence: the driver switches the two coils in order, and each switch moves the rotor one step.

Section 2Microsteps and speed

The TMC2209 can split each full step into smaller ones by setting the two coil currents to in-between values. We use 16 microsteps, so one turn needs:

\[ 200 \times 16 = 3200 \text{ STEP pulses per turn} \qquad f_{\text{STEP}} = \frac{\text{rpm} \times 3200}{60} \]

So 30 rpm needs 1600 pulses per second. The FastAccelStepper library makes these pulses in hardware and speeds up and slows down smoothly ("acceleration"), so the motor doesn't jump or lose steps. It also counts every pulse, which is how g knows how to go back to 0. In T9 the same count lets Reset unwind every turn.

In one sentence: 3200 pulses = one turn, and the pulse count is the motor's position.

Section 3 · the hard ideaThe driver sets current, not voltage

Why this matters: the motor label says 4.1 V, but we feed 24 V. That's correct, and it's safe only because the driver controls current.

The motor's torque comes from the coil current. The driver measures the current through its small sense resistors (0.11 Ω) and switches the 24 V on and off very fast, so the current stays exactly at the set value ("chopper"). The high voltage only helps the current build up quickly at higher speed.

The motor is rated 1 A per phase. We set 700 mA (and half of that when standing still), well below the limit. Heat in the motor is about I² × R per coil: 2 × 0.7² × 4.1 Ω ≈ 4 W. Warm is normal; too hot to touch is not.

We also set I_scale_analog = 0: the current then comes only from software. The blue screw on the driver can no longer change it by accident.

In one sentence: the driver regulates the current to 700 mA, so 24 V is fine for a "4.1 V" motor.

Every half second the program reads the driver's flags (over-temperature, short circuit). If one is set, it switches the motor off at once.

Section 5Why the three safety rules

  • Never unplug the motor while powered. A coil stores energy like a spring. Pulling the plug suddenly creates a large voltage spike that destroys the driver's output transistors.
  • Motor power ON last, OFF first. The ESP32 must already be running (EN held high = driver off) before the driver gets power, so the motor can't jump.
  • Current limit or fuse. If something is wired wrong, the supply limits the damage instead of letting several amps flow.
  • The 10 kΩ pull-up on EN. While the ESP32 boots, its pins float for a moment. The resistor holds EN high = driver off.
  • The 100 µF capacitor. It absorbs fast current pulses and spikes on the 24 V line, right at the driver.
