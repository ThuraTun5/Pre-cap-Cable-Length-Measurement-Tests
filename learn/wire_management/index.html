Wire management: board and jig

After this page you can solder a clean, safe perfboard. You can also plan every cable on the jig so that it does not break, come loose, or add noise to the encoder.

Your answers: one page with two parts · new to soldering · you have solid and stranded wire · electronics box at the fixed-support end.

Your wiring will only grow. T10 already needs three atlas tabs, and the E-stop, the touchscreen and motor 3 come later. This page has two parts: the wires on the soldered board, and the cables across the 1 m jig.

Temperature

SolderStart atRange from sources
Leaded, 63/37 (Sn63Pb37)330 °C320–350 °C

The sources give slightly different numbers. Start at the value in the middle column. If the solder does not flow within 2–3 seconds, go up 10–20 °C. If the board turns brown, go down.

Safety (five rules)

  1. The tip is about 330 °C. Put the iron back in the stand every time. Never catch a falling iron.
  2. Fumes: work with the fan or an open window (Adafruit).
  3. Leaded solder: wash your hands after soldering. No food or drink at the bench.
  4. Glasses on when you cut legs. Point the leg down when you cut.
  5. Switch off and unplug the iron when you leave the bench.

In one sentence: a hot, clean tip, a board that does not move, and a fan are most of good soldering.

Small details
  • Normal rosin flux is "no-clean": you may leave it. Clean it with isopropyl alcohol if it is sticky, or when you want to inspect a joint closely.
  • Leaded or lead-free: your university lab may have a rule. Ask first (Adafruit gives the same advice to students).
  • Do not use a soldering gun, a gas torch or a "cold heat" iron on electronics (Adafruit).

Section 2One good solder joint

Why this matters: Every connection on the board is a joint. One bad joint can make the encoder skip or stop a driver.

The idea in simple words. Solder is not glue. It wets: it flows onto metal that is hot and clean, like water spreading on clean glass. So you heat the metal, and the hot metal melts the solder. The comparison stops here: water spreads on cold glass, but solder does not spread on cold metal. It only makes a ball.

Joint doctor

Click a joint to see what went wrong and how to fix it. Compare your real joints with these.

In one sentence: heat the pad and the leg together, feed solder into them, take the solder away, take the iron away, and keep still. That is 2–4 seconds.

Small details
  • Big metal parts (screw terminals, the 24 V wires, the GND bus) take more heat. Hold the tip longer (3–5 s) or raise the temperature a little. Do not press harder.
  • Wire to pad: tin the wire end first. Strip 3 mm, twist the strands, touch it with the iron and a little solder. Then put it on or through the pad and heat again.
  • Heat shrink goes onto the wire before you solder (see Section 9).
  • Cut legs after soldering, just above the cone. Do not cut into the solder.

How much current does the 24 V wire carry?

The motor coils turn electric power into heat. Each coil has resistance \(R_c = 4.1\ \Omega\) (motor data). The driver keeps \(I = 0.7\ \text{A}\) in each coil (your T7 software current). There are \(n = 4\) coils: 2 motors × 2 coils.

\[ P = n\, I^2 R_c = 4 \times (0.7\ \text{A})^2 \times 4.1\ \Omega \approx 8\ \text{W} \] \[ I_{24} \approx \frac{P}{V} = \frac{8\ \text{W}}{24\ \text{V}} \approx 0.34\ \text{A} \]

The supply current is smaller than the coil current, because the driver chops the 24 V like a step-down converter. Add a little for the drivers: the real value is still well under 1 A. So 22 AWG is plenty for 24 V on the board and in the motor cables.

Section 4Plan the perfboard

Why this matters: On a perfboard you are the PCB designer. Without a plan, wires cross, pads get covered, and later changes become impossible. Your draft in docs/perfboard is a good start; this section gives the rules for turning it into a soldering plan.

Rule 1 · Sockets for every module

The ESP32 and both drivers sit in female headers. The 74LVC245 sits in a DIP socket. A broken part is then a 10-second swap, and the iron never touches the module.

Rule 2 · Two layers

Rule 3 · Quiet area, loud area, small loops

Put the logic (ESP32, 74LVC245, encoder plug) in one area and the 24 V parts (drivers, terminals) in another. Your draft already does this. Then make the loop of the 24 V current small: the + wire and the − wire run side by side.

Why "side by side" works: the field of a wire pair Extra

One wire with current \(I\) makes a magnetic field \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\) at distance \(r\). The return wire carries the same current the other way. When the two wires are close together (spacing \(s\)), their fields almost cancel:

\[ \frac{B_{\text{pair}}}{B_{\text{one}}} = \frac{s\,r}{r^2 - s^2/4} \approx \frac{s}{r} \quad (\text{for } r \gg s) \]

\(s\) = spacing between the + and − wire (mm). \(r\) = distance to the wire that picks up noise (mm). The same rule works both ways: a small loop also picks up less noise. That is why we twist pairs.

Rule 4 · Ground: two returns meet only at the drivers

The motor current returns to the 24 V terminal through its own GND wire. The logic GND bus carries only small currents. In your draft they meet only at the drivers. Keep it that way: the big motor current never flows through the logic GND bus.

Rule 5 · Neat routing

  • Short wires along the grid. Straight lines, 90° bends.
  • No wire over a pad you still need to solder.
  • Leave one empty hole between different nets.
  • Decoupling capacitors right at their pins: 100 nF at the chip, 100 µF at each driver.

Rule 6 · Solder order: low parts first, wires last

How to lay one wire (five steps):

  1. Strip 3 mm from a solid wire.
  2. Push it through the hole from the top.
  3. On the back, bend the end flat towards the next pad of the same net.
  4. Solder the pad. For a bare bus, solder every pad it passes.
  5. Cut the extra end. Beep-test the wire before the next one.

In one sentence: sockets for the modules, insulated wires on top and bare wires on the back, a quiet area and a loud area, and the 24 V + and − always side by side.

Small details
  • Female headers: plug the module in to keep the headers straight. Solder two corner pins, unplug the module, then solder the rest.
  • From your draft: driver 2's GND wire runs up the right edge (column 58), while its +24 V runs up column 49. The loop between them is about 2 cm × 5.5 cm. In the hole-by-hole plan we can run them side by side.
  • Test points: leave a short bare loop of wire on 3V3, 5V, GND and 24 V. Then the meter probes have something safe to touch.
  • Reserved area: keep it empty, and let the 3V3 and GND buses end near it, so the E-stop, touchscreen and motor 3 can connect later.

Part 2 · Across the jig

Section 6The cable map

Why this matters: Seven cables will leave the box. Without a map they become a knot that nobody can fix or explain to the next team.

Your box sits at the fixed-support end. So motor 1's cable (C3) is short and never moves. Motor 2 (C4) and the encoder (C5) ride on the slider: their cables are long and they move. Click a cable.

The cables fall into three families: power (C2), motor (C3, C4, later C6; these are loud) and signal (C1, C5, later C7; these are quiet). The rule for the whole jig: loud cables and quiet cables take different paths.

In one sentence: give every cable an ID, a route and a family before you cut anything; with the box at the fixed end, only C4 and C5 move.

Section 7Noise: keep quiet wires away from loud ones

Why this matters: Your T10 noise check asks one question: do the motor wires make the encoder count while nothing moves? On the finished jig, the answer depends mostly on how you route the cables.

The idea in simple words. The driver switches 24 V on and off thousands of times per second, and each switch is very fast. Noise can jump into a neighbour wire in two ways:

  • Electric (capacitive): two wires side by side act like a tiny capacitor. Every fast voltage jump pushes a small current through it into the neighbour wire. This is the main problem for our encoder (see Worked example 2).
  • Magnetic: current in a loop makes a magnetic field, and a nearby loop picks it up. Small loops make and pick up less (see the field of a wire pair in Section 4).

Rules for this jig

  1. Motor cables and the encoder cable take separate paths, a few centimetres or more apart, with separate ties.
  2. If they must cross, cross at 90°.
  3. In the drag chain they must share space: put them at opposite sides of the chain (use a separator if the chain has one), and use a shielded encoder cable.
  4. Keep each motor coil's two wires together (A1 + A2, B1 + B2), twisted if you make the cable yourself.
  5. Encoder cable: shield connected to GND at the box end only. Inside, pair each signal with a GND wire.

In one sentence: loud and quiet cables on separate paths, crossing at 90°, and a shielded encoder cable grounded at the box end only.

Small details
  • The firmware also helps. T10 sets the encoder filter to 100 clock ticks at 80 MHz, so the counter ignores pulses shorter than 1.25 µs. Good wiring is still the first defence: bursts and ringing can be longer than that.
  • If you use network cable for the encoder, choose stranded patch cable (it bends), with a shield (S/FTP), not solid installation cable.
  • The stock motor cable is a flat ribbon with coil A and coil B side by side. For 1–2 m this is normally fine (3D printers use it). If a motor runs rough with a long cable, Analog Devices (Trinamic) suggests shielded twisted pairs, or a GND wire between the two coil pairs.
  • Keep cables only as long as needed. A big coil of spare cable is a big loop.

Section 8 · the hard ideaMoving cables

Why this matters: A cable that bends again and again breaks inside its insulation. You see nothing from outside. The encoder just starts to skip at some slider positions, or a motor coil opens.

The idea in simple words. Bend a paper clip back and forth: it breaks at the bend after a few times. Copper does the same, just more slowly (metal fatigue). Two things help: many thin strands instead of one solid core, and a gentle bend (big radius) that always happens in the same controlled way. The comparison stops here: a paper clip is one solid wire; a good flexible cable is more like a rope.

The math of a drag chain

Fix one end of the chain to the base, and the other end to the slider. igus (a chain maker) gives the chain length:

\[ L_K = \frac{S}{2} + K \qquad \text{(fixed end in the middle of the travel)} \] \[ L_K = \frac{S}{2} + \Delta M + K \qquad \text{(fixed end away from the middle)} \] \[ K = \pi R + 2T \]

and every cable inside needs a bend radius that is big enough:

\[ R \ \ge\ R_{\min} = k \cdot d \]

Why the fixed end goes in the middle: the U-bend moves at half the slider's speed, so the chain only has to cover half the travel each way. With the fixed end at one end of the travel, \(\Delta M = S/2\) and the chain becomes \(S + K\) long: about 70 % longer for your jig.

Rules inside the chain

  • Cables lie loose, side by side. Never twisted, never tied together inside.
  • Tie each cable firmly at both chain ends. Then the bend always happens inside the chain.
  • No joint, splice or plug inside the chain.
  • Motor cable on one side, encoder cable on the other side.

In one sentence: moving cables must be flexible and must bend in a controlled way, so put motor 2 and the encoder cable in a drag chain with its fixed end in the middle, and check R ≥ k·d.

Small details
  • A flat ribbon (like the stock motor cable) bends easily across its flat side. Lay it in the chain so it bends that way. For a flat cable, \(d\) is its thickness.
  • Check that the chain never touches the rotating clamp heads, and that it stays out of the camera's view.
  • You move the slider by hand, slowly and not very often, compared with a machine. A normal flexible cable with a good radius should last. Solid wire never will.

Section 9Cable ends: plugs, terminals, splices

Why this matters: Most cable failures happen at the ends, not in the middle.

Rules

  • Stranded wire into a screw terminal: crimp a ferrule on it. Second choice: twist the bare strands and tighten again after a week. Never tin it.
  • Plugs where you take the jig apart: at motor 2 and the encoder on the slider. Use different pin counts for different jobs (motor 4 pins, encoder 5 pins) so they cannot be swapped. Motor 1 and motor 2 use the same plug: label them.
  • Never unplug a motor while the drivers have 24 V (SAFETY 4.1). So a motor plug must not fall out by itself. The loose Dupont plug on the stock cable is not good enough; use the screw terminals from your draft, or a locking plug.
  • No solder joints in a moving part of a cable.

A wire splice (five steps):

  1. Slide the heat shrink onto one wire, far from the joint.
  2. Strip 6–8 mm from both wires and twist the strands together.
  3. Heat from below and feed solder until it flows through the strands.
  4. Let it cool without moving.
  5. Slide the tube over the joint and shrink it.

In one sentence: ferrules in screw terminals, locking or screwed motor connections, different plugs for different jobs, and heat shrink on before you solder.

Small details
  • Ferrule sizes: 0.25 mm² for 24 AWG, 0.34 mm² for 22 AWG, 0.5 mm² for 20 AWG (compare the areas in Section 3). For thinner wire, fold the strands double before you crimp.
  • The encoder's own plug is a Molex 50-57-9405 housing (5 pins, 2.54 mm), per the AMT10 datasheet.
  • A ferrule needs a ferrule crimping tool; pliers do not press it evenly.

Section 10Strain relief and fixing

Why this matters: Someone will pull a cable: you, a teammate, or the slider. A tie decides whether the pull lands on plastic or on a solder joint.

  • Cables enter the box through a grommet or a cable gland. No sharp metal or wood edges.
  • Cable ties firm, not crushing. The insulation must not be dented (NASA workmanship rule).
  • Tie the USB cable to the box within a few cm of the ESP32, so the ESP32's USB socket never takes a pull.
  • Hot glue only as a last resort, and never over pads or bare joints.
  • Keep every cable away from the rotating clamp heads and out of the camera's view.

In one sentence: a tie near every end takes the pull, so joints and plugs never do.

Section 12Assembly order and final test

Build (five steps)

  1. Mount the box, the supports and the drag chain.
  2. Lay the fixed cables (C1, C2, C3) and tie them.
  3. Pull C4 and C5 through the chain. Tie them at both chain ends.
  4. Fit the ends (ferrules, plugs) and the labels.
  5. Beep-test every cable end to end before it goes into the board.

Test (five steps)

  1. Power off: move the slider slowly over the full travel. Nothing pulls tight, rubs, or enters the camera view.
  2. Power up in order: USB first, 24 V last.
  3. T10 noise check on the real jig: encoder still, motors running 1 minute, no pos lines.
  4. Move the slider while the motors run: the length changes smoothly.
  5. Pull gently on each cable at the box: the tie takes it.

In one sentence: build fixed cables first and moving cables second, test every cable before it is connected, then repeat the T10 noise check on the real jig.

Example 2 · Why can a motor wire disturb the encoder?

Given: a motor wire and the encoder A wire tied together for 1 m. Both 24 AWG (copper radius \(a = 0.255\) mm), centres \(D = 2\) mm apart. The driver switches 24 V in about 100 ns (an assumption, for the order of size). AMT10 datasheet: CMOS output, 2 mA, output high at least \(V_{DD} - 0.8\) V. 74LVC245 input: HIGH above \(V_{IH} = 2.0\) V. Find: how big the noise spike is.

1
Why: two parallel wires form a small capacitor.\(C' = \dfrac{\pi \varepsilon_0}{\operatorname{arcosh}(D/2a)} = \dfrac{\pi \cdot 8.854\times10^{-12}}{\operatorname{arcosh}(3.92)} = \dfrac{2.78\times10^{-11}}{2.04} \approx 13.6\) pF per metre (in air; insulation makes it a little bigger).

Why: the pushed current flows through that resistance.\(V_{\text{spike}} \approx i \cdot R_{\text{out}} = 3.3\ \text{mA} \cdot 400\ \Omega \approx 1.3\) V on top of a LOW signal.
5
Why: compare with the chip's threshold.A LOW can sit up to 0.8 V, so \(0.8 + 1.3 = 2.1\) V \(> 2.0\) V: the 74LVC245 can read a false HIGH. With 2 m tied together the spike doubles.

Check: F × V/s = A ✓. The spike current (3.3 mA) is bigger than what the encoder output is made for (2 mA), so the result is believable. Fixes: separate the cables (less capacitance, and other grounded parts take some of the field), and use a grounded shield: it catches this current and sends it to GND. The firmware's 1.25 µs filter ignores one 100 ns spike, but not a burst.

PracticeCode: cable planner

Python, standard library only. Change the numbers at the top to your measured travel and the chain you buy. Run it in the VS Code terminal with python cable_plan.py.

# cable_plan.py - plan the moving cables of the jig (run: python cable_plan.py)
import math

S = 1000                # slider travel in mm (measure yours)
R = 55                  # drag chain bend radius in mm (from the chain's data)
T = 20                  # drag chain pitch = length of one link in mm
FIXED_AT_MIDDLE = True  # is the chain's fixed end in the middle of the travel?
X1 = 100                # box -> start of the travel, in mm
INSIDE = 100            # cable inside the box, up to the board, in mm
SLIDER = 200            # chain's moving end -> part on the slider, in mm
LOOP = 100              # small spare loop at each end, in mm

cables = {              # name: (outer diameter in mm, bend factor k)
    "C4 motor 2": (4.5, 10),
    "C5 encoder": (5.0, 10),
}

K = math.pi * R + 2 * T                  # add-on for the bend (igus)
dM = 0 if FIXED_AT_MIDDLE else S / 2     # how far the fixed end is from the middle
LK = S / 2 + dM + K                      # chain length
links = math.ceil(LK / T)                # round up to whole links
x_fixed = X1 + S / 2 if FIXED_AT_MIDDLE else X1
print(f"Chain: K = {K:.0f} mm, LK = {LK:.0f} mm -> {links} links = {links * T} mm")

for name, (d, k) in cables.items():
    r_min = k * d                        # smallest bend radius this cable allows
    ok = "OK" if R >= r_min else "TOO TIGHT - bigger chain or thinner cable"
    cut = (INSIDE + x_fixed + links * T + SLIDER + 2 * LOOP) * 1.10  # +10 % spare
    print(f"{name}: R_min = {r_min:.0f} mm -> {ok}; cut length = {cut / 1000:.2f} m")

# 5 V drop on the encoder cable: 6 mA, 26 AWG = 0.134 ohm/m, out and back
drop = 0.006 * 0.134 * 2 * cut / 1000
print(f"Encoder 5 V drop: {drop * 1000:.1f} mV (tiny, the encoder needs only 3.6 V)")

Expected output (tested):

Chain: K = 213 mm, LK = 713 mm -> 36 links = 720 mm
C4 motor 2: R_min = 45 mm -> OK; cut length = 2.00 m
C5 encoder: R_min = 50 mm -> OK; cut length = 2.00 m
Encoder 5 V drop: 3.2 mV (tiny, the encoder needs only 3.6 V)

The last line shows something useful: over a 2 m cable the encoder's 5 V drops only 3 mV, because it draws about 6 mA. So wire thickness for the encoder is about strength, not voltage.

PracticeMistakes to avoid

  1. Carrying solder on the tip to the joint. The flux burns off on the way, and the pad never gets hot. Spot it: a dull ball sitting on the leg. Fast check: the pad edge is still bare copper.
  2. Moving the board while the solder freezes. Spot it: grainy, cracked surface. Fix: reheat with a little flux, keep still for 3 s.
  3. Heat shrink forgotten. After soldering, the tube cannot go over the joint anymore. Fast check: before you heat anything, ask "is the tube on?"
  4. Tinned stranded wire in a screw terminal. It feels tight today and is loose in a month. Fast check: after a week, if the screw turns further, the end was creeping. Use a ferrule.
  5. Solid wire in a moving cable. It breaks inside the insulation. Spot it: the encoder skips only at some slider positions. Fast check: beep test while you move the slider.
  6. Motor cable tied to the encoder cable. Spot it: pos lines while the encoder is still (T10 noise check).
  7. Shield connected at both ends, or to 5 V. Fast check: beep test between the shield and GND at the encoder end must be silent with the box end disconnected.
  8. Cables tied together inside the drag chain, or bent tight at the chain ends. Fast check: move the slider by hand; every cable should slide freely inside the chain.
  9. 24 V + and − far apart on the board. Spot it on the drawing: shade the area between them. Big area = big antenna.
  10. A solder blob on the back of the double-sided board shorts a top pad. Fast check: look at the top after each back-side group; beep-test neighbours.
  11. Cables cut exactly to length. No spare to redo an end. Fix: leave about 10 cm spare loops at both ends.
  12. Unplugging a motor while 24 V is on. The voltage spike kills the driver (SAFETY 4.1). Rule: motor power off first, always.
