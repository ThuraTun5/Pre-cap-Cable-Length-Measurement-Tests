Buck converter → 5.0 V

After this page you can explain how the buck converter turns 12 V into 5 V, calculate how much current it takes from the supply, set it safely to 5.0 V, and read the LED codes of the Part B test.

I assumed: you know Ohm's law and P = V·I. Goal = understand T02 before you test it.

Section 1Why a buck converter, not a simple regulator

Why this matters: at the end, the jig runs from a 12–24 V supply (for the motors) without a PC. The ESP32 then needs its own 5 V from that supply.

Both kinds of regulator give 5 V. The difference is where the extra voltage goes.

  • A linear regulator works like a valve that is partly closed. The current is the same in and out, and the extra 7 V (12 − 5) times the current becomes heat.
  • A buck converter works like a tap that is opened fully, then closed fully, very fast. An inductor and a capacitor smooth the pulses into a steady 5 V. Very little energy becomes heat.

\[ P_{\text{loss,linear}} = (V_{in} - V_{out}) \cdot I_{out} \qquad\qquad \eta_{\text{linear}} \approx \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} \]

At 12 V in and 0.4 A out, a linear regulator wastes (12 − 5) × 0.4 = 2.8 W as heat (efficiency about 42 %). A buck converter at about 75 % efficiency wastes about 0.67 W. Try other values in the calculator in section 5.

In one sentence: a buck converter switches instead of burning off the extra voltage, so it stays cool even for big voltage drops.

Section 2How a buck converter works

Why this matters: it explains why the module needs some "headroom" (input above output), and why its output is DC with a tiny ripple.

The switch turns on and off many thousand times per second (the listing for your module says 400 kHz). If it is ON for a fraction D of each cycle, the average voltage after the inductor and capacitor is about D × Vin:

\[ V_{out} \approx D \cdot V_{in} \quad\Rightarrow\quad D \approx \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{5}{12} \approx 0.42 \]

So at 12 V the switch is ON about 42 % of the time. At 24 V it is ON only about 21 % of the time, and the output is still 5 V.

In one sentence: the switch chops the input into pulses, and the inductor and capacitor average them into a steady lower voltage.

Small details D = Vout/Vin is the ideal case; real losses make D a little larger. A buck converter can only go down. It needs some extra input voltage (your module's listing says at least 1 V more at high current; other LM2596 guides say 1.5 V), so 12 V in for 5 V out is comfortable. The listing calls the module an "LM2596S", but it accepts up to 60 V and switches at 400 kHz. A real LM2596 is rated 40 V and 150 kHz, so the chip on your board is probably a different one. That is why we trust the meter, not the label.

Section 3 · the hard ideaFeedback and the trimmer screw

Why this matters: it explains what you change when you turn the blue screw, and why the output stays at 5 V when the input or the load changes.

The converter does not "know" 5 V. It only knows its own small, exact reference voltage (about 1.23 V on an LM2596). Two resistors divide the output down and feed it back. The controller adjusts the duty cycle until the divided output exactly equals the reference.

\[ V_{out} = V_{ref}\left(1 + \frac{R_2}{R_1}\right) \]

The blue trimmer is part of this divider. Turning it changes R2/R1, and so Vout. For 5 V you need R2/R1 = 5/1.23 − 1 ≈ 3.07. The trimmer has many turns (often 15–25), so each turn changes the voltage only a little. This is good for fine setting, but it can feel like "nothing happens" at first.

Because of the feedback, the output stays at 5.0 V when you change the input from 12 V to 24 V, and when the ESP32 suddenly draws more current. This is what the optional 24 V check in Part A shows you.

In one sentence: the chip keeps the divided output equal to its reference, and the trimmer changes the divider, so the trimmer sets the output.

Small details On most modules, turning clockwise raises the voltage. Some modules work the other way, so always watch the meter while turning. Set the voltage with no load first, then check it again under load (Part B).

The board's own 3.3 V regulator is a linear type (a 3-pin part next to pins 12–13). For a linear regulator the current in equals the current out, so the 5 V side carries about the same current as the 3.3 V side, roughly 0.25 A average and 0.4 A peak. The regulator turns (5 − 3.3) × 0.38 ≈ 0.65 W into heat at the peak. That's why it can feel warm.

In one sentence: plan for about 0.4 A at 5 V for the ESP32, the busiest case with Wi-Fi transmitting.

Section 5How much current does the supply give?

Power in = power out + losses. With efficiency η:

\[ I_{in} = \frac{V_{out}\, I_{out}}{\eta \, V_{in}} \]

The input current is smaller than the output current, because the input voltage is higher. A buck converter trades voltage for current.

Section 6Setting it safely (Part A)

Why this matters: a new module can come set to almost any voltage, even close to its input. Connecting the ESP32 before setting it would put 12 V on a 5 V pin.
1
Only the meter on the output. If the voltage is wrong, nothing gets damaged.
2
Low input first (12 V) and a low current limit (0.3 A). If the wiring is wrong, the supply limits the damage.
3
Turn slowly, watch the meter. Stop at 4.95–5.05 V.
4
OFF → ON check. The value must come back the same, so you know the setting holds.

The pads are labelled IN+, IN−, OUT+, OUT−. Solder short wires to them (24 AWG red/black came with the kit). Crocodile clips are only for a quick test: they slip and can bridge two pads.

In one sentence: set the voltage with nothing to damage, then prove it holds, and only then connect a real load.

Section 7Part B: the ESP32 as a load, and brown-out

Why this matters: a voltage that's correct with no load can still dip under a real, pulsing load. Part B tests the real situation.

In Part B the USB cable is unplugged, so there is no serial monitor. The program therefore reports with LEDs.

The ESP32 has a brown-out detector. If its supply dips too low, it resets itself instead of running wrongly. After any reset, the chip remembers why it reset. The program reads this with esp_reset_reason() and shows it on the RGB LED for 2 s:

RGB at start-upReset reasonMeaning
WhiteESP_RST_POWERONNormal power-on (or the EN button). Good.
RedESP_RST_BROWNOUTThe voltage dipped too low. Check the setting, the wires and the current limit.
Yellowanything elseCrash, watchdog, etc. Plug in USB and read the serial monitor.

To load the supply, the program starts a Wi-Fi scan every 4 seconds. It uses WiFi.scanNetworks(true): the true means "scan in the background". So loop() never waits and the blue LED keeps blinking at a steady 1 Hz. That is the same non-blocking idea you learned in T01. If the blue LED ever stops or the RGB shows red at start-up, the supply is not good enough.

Why USB must be unplugged: the buck's 5 V and the PC's 5 V would be joined on the board's 5 V line. We can't confirm this board has a protection diode, so the higher of the two could push current into the other (see the T01 page, section 8).

In one sentence: Part B runs the ESP32 at its busiest from the buck alone, and the start-up colour tells you if the voltage ever dipped.

PracticeWorked example: choose the current limit for Part B

Problem. The supply is set to 12 V. The ESP32 draws up to 0.40 A at 5 V. Assume the buck efficiency is 75 %. (a) What duty cycle does the buck run at? (b) How much current does the supply give at the peak? (c) What current limit should you set? (d) How much heat would a linear regulator make instead?

a

Why: for an ideal buck converter, the duty cycle is the voltage ratio.

\( D = \dfrac{5\ \text{V}}{12\ \text{V}} = 0.42 \) (42 % ON time)

b

Why: first the output power, then add the losses, then divide by the input voltage.

\( P_{out} = 5\ \text{V} \times 0.40\ \text{A} = 2.0\ \text{W} \qquad P_{in} = \dfrac{2.0\ \text{W}}{0.75} = 2.67\ \text{W} \qquad I_{in} = \dfrac{2.67\ \text{W}}{12\ \text{V}} = 0.22\ \text{A} \)

c

Why: the limit must be above the normal peak, so the ESP32 is not starved, but low enough to protect against a short circuit.

0.22 A peak → choose 0.5 A. That is more than twice the peak, and still far below the supply's 3 A.

d

Why: a linear regulator turns the whole voltage difference into heat.

\( P_{loss} = (12 - 5)\ \text{V} \times 0.40\ \text{A} = 2.8\ \text{W} \) compared with \( 2.67 - 2.0 = 0.67\ \text{W} \) for the buck.

Sanity check. Units: W / V = A ✓. The supply current (0.22 A) is smaller than the output current (0.40 A), which must be true for a step-down converter. At 24 V the supply current halves (≈0.11 A), as expected for the same power.

Hands-onThe Part B code

The full file is tests/T02_buck/src/main.cpp. It compiled with PlatformIO: SUCCESS, no warnings, static check passed. This is the core of loop():

// Start a Wi-Fi scan every 4 s. "true" = background (asynchronous) scan,
// so loop() never waits.
if (!scanRunning && now - lastScanMs >= SCAN_INTERVAL_MS) {
  lastScanMs = now;
  WiFi.scanNetworks(true);
  scanRunning = true;
}

// Has the background scan finished?
// scanComplete(): -1 = still running, -2 = failed, >= 0 = networks found
if (scanRunning) {
  int16_t result = WiFi.scanComplete();
  if (result >= 0 || result == WIFI_SCAN_FAILED) {
    scanRunning = false;
    int found = result > 0 ? result : 0;
    if (found > 0) setRgb(0, RGB_LEVEL, 0);   // green flash
    else           setRgb(RGB_LEVEL, 0, 0);   // red flash
    flashOn = true;
    flashStartMs = now;
    WiFi.scanDelete();                       // free the memory
  }
}

And the start-up check in setup():

esp_reset_reason_t reason = esp_reset_reason();   // why did we (re)start?
if (reason == ESP_RST_POWERON)       setRgb(RGB_LEVEL, RGB_LEVEL, RGB_LEVEL);  // white
else if (reason == ESP_RST_BROWNOUT) setRgb(RGB_LEVEL, 0, 0);                  // red
else                                 setRgb(RGB_LEVEL, RGB_LEVEL, 0);          // yellow
delay(BOOT_SIGNAL_MS);   // OK here: setup() runs once, before the work starts

Watch outTest traps

  • Meter probe in the "A" (current) socket. In the current socket the meter is almost a short circuit. On the output, it shorts the converter. For voltage, the red probe goes in the V/Ω socket.
  • Setting the voltage with the ESP32 already connected. A new module may output 10+ V. Always set it with only the meter connected.
  • "Turning does nothing". It's a multi-turn trimmer: keep turning (up to ~20 turns) while watching the meter. If the voltage never moves, check that the input really is above 6 V.
  • Input too close to the output. With 6 V in, the output can't hold 5.0 V under load. Use 12 V.
  • IN and OUT swapped. The pads are on opposite ends. The supply goes to IN+/IN−, never to OUT.
  • USB still plugged in during Part B. Two 5 V sources joined. Unplug USB first.
  • OUT+ on a 3.3V pin. 5 V on the 3.3 V line damages the ESP32. The board's 5V pins are at the bottom of the left row.
  • Crocodile clips touching. Two clips on neighbouring pads can short. Solder wires, or angle the clips apart.
